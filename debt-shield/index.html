    The rules

    Your rights under Virginia and federal law

    Debt settlement companies serving Virginians answer to both. Where the rules overlap, the company has to meet the stricter one.

    VA Virginia

    A license from the State Corporation Commission

    Anyone providing debt settlement services to Virginia residents must be licensed by the SCC's Bureau of Financial Institutions — even with no office in Virginia. The license requires a surety bond of $25,000 to $350,000, annual reports, and examinations.

    Exempt: banks, savings institutions, credit unions, and attorneys licensed to practice law in Virginia. Nonprofit credit counseling agencies that run debt management plans are licensed separately under Chapter 20.

    Va. Code §§ 6.2-2027, 6.2-2029, 6.2-2035 · 10VAC5-230
    A fee cap — and nothing else on top

    A licensee may charge a total fee of either

    • no more than 20% of the principal of the debt you enrolled, or
    • no more than 30% of the difference between what you owed when the debt was settled and what you pay to satisfy it.

    With several debts in one agreement, the 20% fee is earned debt by debt, in proportion to each debt's share of the total. The company may not charge or receive any other fee from you for debt settlement services.

    Va. Code § 6.2-2041(A)–(C)
    No fee until a debt is actually settled

    A licensee may not request or receive payment until it has settled, reduced, or otherwise changed the terms of at least one of your debts under an agreement you signed — mirroring the federal advance-fee ban.

    Va. Code § 6.2-2040
    A written agreement you can walk away from

    You must get a signed, dated written agreement, and a duplicate original once both sides sign. It must state that you can end the arrangement at any time, for any reason, and explain how disputes are resolved. Your funds must be held at an FDIC-insured institution, and the company cannot make you sign a power of attorney to enroll.

    Va. Code § 6.2-2040
    Honest advertising

    False, misleading, or deceptive advertising is prohibited, and SCC regulations add rules on how licensees advertise and on referral arrangements with affiliates.

    Va. Code § 6.2-2042 · 10VAC5-230
    What you can do if the rules are broken

    A violation of Chapter 20.1 is a prohibited practice under the Virginia Consumer Protection Act. You may sue for actual damages or $500, whichever is greater — up to three times actual damages or $1,000 if the violation was willful — plus reasonable attorney fees. The SCC can also issue cease-and-desist orders, impose civil penalties, and revoke licenses.

    Va. Code §§ 59.1-200, 59.1-204 · Chapter 20.1
    Time limits on old debts

    Virginia generally gives creditors 5 years to sue on a written contract and 3 years on an unwritten one, from the date of breach. A debt past that window may still be collected but can't be won in court — worth knowing before you pay to settle it. Get legal advice: payments and written acknowledgments can affect the clock.

    Va. Code § 8.01-246

    US Federal

    The advance-fee ban

    A debt relief company that sells by phone may not request or receive any fee until (1) it has settled or changed the terms of at least one debt, (2) you have signed that settlement, and (3) you have made at least one payment under it. Each fee must be either proportional to that debt's share of your total enrolled debt, or a fixed percentage of the amount saved — measured from the balance when you enrolled.

    This covers calls you make in response to ads, not just cold calls. In-person sales are exempt, which is one reason Virginia's own rule matters.

    Telemarketing Sales Rule, 16 CFR 310.4(a)(5)(i), 310.6(b)(5)–(6)
    Your dedicated savings account

    The company may ask you to save in a dedicated account only if: it's at an insured financial institution; you own the funds and any interest; the account administrator is not owned by, controlled by, or affiliated with the company; the company pays and takes no referral money from the administrator; and you can withdraw at any time without penalty, getting your money (minus fees already earned) within 7 business days.

    16 CFR 310.4(a)(5)(ii)
    What they must tell you before you enroll
    • How long it will take to get results, and when they'll make a settlement offer to each creditor.
    • How much you must save before they make each offer.
    • That if you stop paying creditors, your credit may suffer, you may be sent to collections or sued, and fees and interest may grow what you owe.
    • That you own the money in your account and can withdraw it at any time.
    • The total cost of the service.
    16 CFR 310.3(a)(1)(i), (viii)
    No misleading promises

    It's illegal to misrepresent how much you'll save, how long it will take, how much you must save before offers are made, the effect on collection calls or lawsuits, the share of customers who get the advertised results, or whether the company is a nonprofit.

    16 CFR 310.3(a)(2)(x) · FTC Act § 5, 15 U.S.C. 45 · CFPA, 12 U.S.C. 5531, 5536
    Credit repair promises bring extra rules

    If a company says it will improve your credit report or score, the Credit Repair Organizations Act may also apply: no payment before services are fully performed, a written contract with required disclosures, and a 3-business-day right to cancel.

    15 U.S.C. 1679b, 1679d, 1679e
    Forgiven debt can be taxable

    Debt a creditor forgives is generally taxable income. Creditors that cancel $600 or more must send you Form 1099-C. You may exclude it to the extent you were insolvent right before the cancellation (Form 982, IRS Publication 4681). Virginia starts from federal adjusted gross income, so the same amount usually flows into your state return.

    26 U.S.C. 61(a)(11), 108, 6050P
    Collectors still have to follow the rules

    While you're in a program, third-party debt collectors are still bound by the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act: no harassment, no false threats, and you can dispute a debt in writing and ask them to stop contacting you.

    15 U.S.C. 1692 et seq. · Regulation F, 12 CFR 1006

    Before you enroll, compare alternatives. A nonprofit credit counseling agency can set up a debt management plan that usually keeps your accounts current, and a Virginia consumer or bankruptcy attorney can tell you whether bankruptcy or a statute-of-limitations defense fits better. Settlement typically requires you to stop paying creditors, which damages credit and invites lawsuits.

    Questions

    Is a "Shield score" a legal finding?

    No. It's a screening tool built from your answers. It points to rules that may have been broken so you know what to ask about and where to report. Only a regulator or a court can decide that a law was violated.

    The company says it's run by attorneys. Does Virginia's law still apply?

    Virginia's Chapter 20.1 exempts persons licensed to practice law in Virginia. A firm with out-of-state lawyers, or a sales company that refers you to an attorney network, may not qualify — ask which Virginia-licensed attorney is handling your file. The federal Telemarketing Sales Rule can still apply to attorney-affiliated programs that sell by phone.

    Why does the settlement check use two different "savings" numbers?

    The federal rule measures savings from what you owed when you enrolled. Virginia's 30% option measures from what you owed when the debt was settled, which is often higher after interest and late fees. Debt Shield checks both; the real savings figure always compares against your enrolled balance, because that's what you would have paid if you'd never joined.

    Does Debt Shield store or send my information?

    No. Everything runs in your browser. Your answers are saved only in this browser so you can come back to them; use "Clear answers" to remove them.
